Peer Review in the Age of AI

October 20, 2026

Recently, a few conversations have turned to what I would study if I were starting a PhD now, or whether I would do a PhD at all. Especially in ML, the way we form hypotheses and run experiments has been changing. So I don’t think it’s a good idea to casually choose a research topic or give people advice about one.

Related to that, I’m interested in what happens to papers and the culture of peer review. As many people, including me, expected, an incredible number of papers are being submitted to arXiv and conferences every day. It’s getting hard to tell who is writing them and who is reviewing them. Still, I enjoy reading papers when I review for journals. It feels like I’m doing something human. That said, now that the barrier to writing a paper is lower, I think we need to stop and consider again what papers exist for, and for whom.

Papers were supposed to function as a medium for sharing scientific theories and hypotheses among people. With that in mind, let’s think about what they offer reviewers, researchers themselves, and AI.

Papers for Reviewers

This goes without saying, but papers let us learn new information and hypotheses, bring new angles to our own research, and develop it further. But this information-gathering role is often being taken over by AI.

Papers for AI

Here, the purpose is simply to gather information. What matters is whether an agent can efficiently get the information it wants from the paper. Rather than readability, it helps to have high information density, with a lot of information organized into a small number of tokens.

Putting it that way might suggest that papers written for humans should be more verbose. That’s not necessarily what I mean. I think a beautiful paper should be short, with only the necessary information laid out. But there should still be care for readability: sentence length, how sentences connect, and so on. My impression is that AI doesn’t care as much about those things.

Papers for the People Writing Them

Anyone who has written a paper will probably understand this: sometimes you understand something more deeply as you write. I think that process is very important. If AI writes the entire paper, much of that process gets skipped, which I think is very dangerous.

With all that in mind, perhaps papers will end up with a two-part format: a main text intended for humans, something like an extended abstract, and an information-dense extended appendix intended for AI.

A recent paper in Nature described a way to turn papers into agents. If the necessary information can be retrieved through queries like this, perhaps we wouldn’t all have to keep feeding the same paper into our own AI.

Actually, I think this is similar to a conference poster session. A person reads the broad outline, and if they want to know more, they can ask the presenter for just the details they want. It could save tokens and collect useful feedback on the paper. That seems efficient to me.

I don’t know whether things will really go this way. But at the very least, I want PhD researchers to get to enjoy writing and reading papers. There’s a lot you understand through writing, and I really don’t want that culture to fade away.
